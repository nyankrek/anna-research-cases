Finding a language for how a product feels

How do you ask people what a product feels like, beyond whether they can use it?

Role
Research design & facilitation
Methods
Toolkit adaptation, workshops, surveys
Output
An adapted desirability word list

01 / The question

The words needed research, too.

Product teams wanted to understand whether their interfaces felt old, complicated or appealing. A translated version of the Microsoft Desirability Toolkit was already in use, but its terms had not been adapted or tested for this Russian-language product context.

Behavioral metrics and task success showed what people did. They said less about the qualities people associated with the product.

02 / The approach

Build the vocabulary with people, then test it.

I reviewed the original toolkit, organised the adaptation process and ran two survey iterations. Designers and a UX writer helped select terms. Users described the best and worst social networks; active users then used the refined list to describe our product and three competitors.

  1. Translate and classify the original terms
  2. Review relevance with design and writing partners
  3. Narrow positive and negative terms with users
  4. Add neutral terms and compare four products

03 / What came out of it

A more useful research instrument.

The work produced a word list better suited to the audience, language and product. Testing it with users and colleagues gave teams a more consistent way to explore emotional and aesthetic responses in future research.
